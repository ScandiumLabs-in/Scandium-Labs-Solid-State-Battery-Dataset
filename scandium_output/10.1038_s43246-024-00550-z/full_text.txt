All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) are promising alternatives to conventional lithium-ion batteries.
ASSBs consist of solid-fast-ion-conducting electrolytes and electrodes that offer improved energy
density, battery safety, speciﬁc power, and fast-charging capability. Despite decades of intensive
research, only a few have high ionic conductivity at ambient temperature. Developing fast ion-
conducting materials requires both synthesis of high-conducting materials and a fundamental
understanding of ion transport mechanisms. However, this is challenging due to wide variations of the
ionic conductivity, even within the same class of materials, indicating the strong inﬂuence of structural
modiﬁcations on ion transport. This Review discusses three selected material classes, namely layered
oxides, polyhedral connections, and cluster anion types, as promising fast ion conductors. Emphasis
is placed on the inherent challenges and the role of the framework structure on mobile ion conduction.
We elucidate strategies to address these challenges by leveraging theoretical frameworks and
insights from materials science.
Solid fast-ion conductors represent a distinct class of ion-conducting
materials characterized by the diffusion of a speciﬁc type of atom through
the interstitial spaces within a solid lattice while other atoms remain
translationally immobile at their crystal sites. This unique behavior allows
liquid-like fast ion conduction of the particular atom within a solid fra-
mework. Notably, solid fast-ion conductors (SFICs) exhibit comparable
ionic conduction to the liquid electrolyte solution, particularly those in
organic solvents1, such as 25 mS cm−1 for Li9.54Si1.74P1.44S11.7Cl0.3
2, and
70 mS cm−1 for Na2(CB9H10)(CB11H12)3 at room temperature (RT).
Additionally, these materials have extended operating temperature range
comparedtotheirliquidcounterparts4–8,higherenergydensitywhileusedin
battery, longer life cycles, and enhanced safety due to the absence of organic
liquid or polymer electrolytes9–12. The technological applications of SFIC are
of signiﬁcant interest, particularly in the development of all-solid-state
batteries (ASSBs), fuel cells, and gas sensors2,13,14. Recently, SFICs have also
been a promising candidate in solid-state barocaloric cooling/heating
applications15–17. ASSBs, in particular, are considered promising candidates
for high-energy-density batteries, addressing the growing demand in the
electric vehicle market as conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs)
approach the theoretical limit9. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs), another
important application, can also beneﬁt from the utilization of SFICs.
Operating at elevated temperatures, SOFCs require stable and efﬁcient
materials to ensure optimal performance.
Conducting ions in a solid phase faces various challenges due to the
dense packing of atoms within the crystal structure, which limits the
availability of space for ionic diffusion. As a result, the ionic conductivity in
solids is generally low. The development of SFICs has witnessed notable
progress18,19 in synthesizing new materials (Fig. 1a). Researchers have
exploreda diverse range of mobile ionic species, including but not limitedto
Li+, Na+, H+, Ag+, Cu+, F−, and H−to enhance the performance of SFICs.
Recent reviews have extensively documented and summarized this
progress4,20–25. In addition to materials that conduct monovalent ions, there
is growing interest in the potential of divalent-cation-conducting materials,
such as Mg2+, Ca2+, and Zn2+ for various energy-storage applications7,26,27.
These materials garner attention due to their signiﬁcant promise in energy-
storage devices, primarily attributed to their higher energy densities when
compared to ASSBs constructed using monovalent cations. Remarkably,
ions such as O2−and Mg2+ have demonstrated notable ionic conductivity,
achieving an impressive value up to 0.1 mS cm−1 at RT7. Among the various
typesofionicconductors,particularemphasishasbeenplacedonLi-ionand

contrast, in crystalline solids, the conduction of ions requires passing
through periodic BN points, creating an energy barrier between two local
energy minima. The conductivity of ions in liquid electrolytes can be

Fast molecule-ion exchange is necessary for fast ion conduction in
liquid electrolytes. In contrast, in crystalline solids, the diffusion of mobile
species, such as lithium ions, encounters periodic BN points offered by
immobile framework species. These points deﬁne energetic barriers that
separate two local minima,typicallyrepresentedby crystallographic sites for
the ions. The energy barrier, also known as migration energy (Em), greatly
inﬂuences ionic conductivity within the solid. Lower migration energies
correspond to higher ionic mobility and conductivity. Understanding these
differences is essential for designing and optimizing solid-state conductors
for various applications.

Major classes of fast ion conductors
The translationally immobile framework anion plays a huge role in cationic
conduction. Therefore, we classiﬁed the fast ion conductors based on their
framework structures: layered oxide materials, polyhedral connecting fra-
mework materials, and cluster anion-type materials. Each class is unique,
and they have a distinct role in cation conduction.
Layered oxide materials
Layered oxide materials are a class of solid fast ion conductors that have a
distinctive structure consisting of mobile cations sandwiched between rigid
frameworklayers.Thesematerialsexhibithighionicconductivity,especially
for alkali metal ions, and have potential applications in electrochemical
devices. Two importantsub-classesoflayeredoxide materialsareβ-alumina
and honeycomb layered oxide.
β-alumina. In the middle of thetwentieth century, a remarkable scientiﬁc
advancement emerged in the ﬁeld of SFICs with the groundbreaking
discovery of β-alumina by Yao and Kummer30. One of the most striking

(3.5 × 10−2 S cm−166 at 25 ∘C), which rivals conventional liquid electro-

attention from researchers in the ﬁeld of solid-state ionics. Notably, β-
alumina also demonstrates fast ion conduction for divalent and even
trivalent cation67. This characteristic expands its potential applications
and highlights its versatility as a solid-state ionic conductor. The ability of
β-alumina to facilitate the rapid conduction of alkali metal ions is
attributed to its unique crystal structure. This structure comprises
interconnected channels or tunnels that enable the efﬁcient movement of
ions, enhancing their conductivity.
Since the discovery of β-alumina, extensive experimental68–71 and
theoretical67,72–75 research efforts have been dedicated to gaining deeper
insights into its ion transport properties and stability. Moreover, synthe-
sizing these materials is straightforward, allowing researchers to obtain
high-quality samples for study and characterization. The general formula of
β-alumina is M2O.xAl2O3 (where M = Li, Na, Ag, K, and Rb)30. This
materialconsistsoftwodistinctsubgroups:β0 andβ″,eachcharacterizedbya
unique structure (Fig. 3a, e). The β″ type exhibits higher ionic conductivity
due to its distinctive framework structure, accommodating a larger number
of vacant cationic sites. The crystal structure of β-alumina comprises
compact layers of spinel blocks formed by Al3+ tetrahedra and octahedra

mobile cations, with oxygen bridges connecting them to form a 3D hex-

while for β″-alumina, it is R3m. The layer between the rigid spinel blocks is
often called the conduction layer (Fig. 3a, e). Within the conduction layer,
three different cationic sites exist: anti-Beevers-Ross (aBR), Beevers-Ross
(BR), and mid-Oxygen (mO) (Fig. 3d)68. The sites are different based on the
local oxygen environment, such as BR is formed by three top and three
bottom oxygens, and aBR is formed by two oxygens (one top and one
bottom).Thesesitesare crucialinfacilitatingcationicdiffusionand hopping
within the material, as presented in Fig. 3b. Occupancy at these sites and the

insights into the diffusion of Na+ ions, including correlation effects and the
presence of mobile and immobile Na+ ions in the diffusion mechanism.

oxygen ions (O2−) through the crystal lattice, utilizing oxygen vacancies as
diffusion paths. In β″-alumina, the conduction mechanism revolves around
the migration of sodium ions (Na+) through the crystal lattice, facilitated by

Honeycomb layered oxides. Honeycomb Layered oxide solids have
attracted considerable attention as potential electrode for sodium-ion
batteries since NaxCoO2 was ﬁrst introduced as a cathode material86.
However, the cation diffusion mechanism is similar to the other elec-
trolytes. Typical layered honeycomb oxides consist of alkali or coinage
metal atoms sandwiched between slabs made exclusively of transition
metal and chalcogen (or pnictogen) atoms arranged in edge-shared
octahedra in a honeycomb fashion (Fig. 3f). These materials offer several
advantages, such as wider interstitial spaces between two-dimensional
polyhedral framework layers that facilitate cation migration, enabling
high ionic conductivity at RT, and excellent chemical and ambient
stability87. Meanwhile, a rigid octahedral framework layer completely
hinders diffusion along the crystalline c-direction. The materials are
classiﬁed based on the local cationic environment, with oxygen (tetra-
hedral, octahedral, or prismatic) sharing rectangular faces, thereby
creating a wide BN for facile cation transport88. These layered honeycomb
oxide structures differ from the β-alumina structure, where oxygen atoms
are located in the conduction layer. Van der Waals forces weakly connect
the framework layers in these honeycomb oxides, and the inter-layer
distance can be adjusted by framework modiﬁcations or a larger size
cationic substitution. Alkali ion conductivity is signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced
by the inter-slab distance, cationic concentration, and their different
octahedral arrangements in the framework layer.
Several experimental and theoretical studies, including MD simula-
tions and topological analysis, have been conducted to gain insights into
alkaline ion conduction and material stability mechanisms88–90. Evstigneeva
et al. synthesized a series of honeycomb layered materials, Na2M2TeO6
(where M = Ni, Zn, Mg, and Co), and discovered surprisingly high ionic
conductivity88. The materials containing nickel (Ni) are well-suited for
utilization as cathode materials, whereas materials substituted with zinc
(Zn) and magnesium (Mg), offering a low electronic conductivity, are
particularly suitable for serving as electrolytes, demonstrating noteworthy
Na+ ion conductivities of 5.7 × 10−4 S cm−1 and 2.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at RT,
respectively91,92. Further investigations, focusing on the impact of homo-
valent calcium ion (Ca2+) substitution and heterovalent gallium ion (Ga3+)

Despite several improvements in materials synthesis, the funda-
mental understanding was lacking. Taking a broader perspective, MD
simulations of Na2Ni2TeO6 aim to understand the mechanism of sodium
ion transport in honeycomb layered oxide materials. Sau and Kumar89
proposed interatomic potential for performing force-ﬁeld-based MD
simulations on the series of honeycomb layered oxides (Na2M2TeO6).
The force ﬁeld accurately reproduced the structural and dynamical
properties observed experimentally. The MD studies revealed detailed
cationic diffusion pathways, energy barriers along the diffusion path
(Fig. 3f), and cationic migration mechanisms. The crystal structure of
Na2Ni2TeO6 consists of three distinct cationic sites: 6g, 4c, and 2a, which
are located at the center of trigonal bi-prisms formed by face- or edge-
sharing NiO6 or TeO6 octahedra. The 2a site is energetically less favor-
able due to the strong electrostatic repulsion between the Na+ ion and the
Te6+ ion in the adjacent TeO6 octahedra. MD simulations revealed that
the Na+ ions preferentially occupy the 6g and 4c sites, in agreement with
the experimental ﬁndings of ref. 95. The 2a site becomes accessible only
at temperatures above 500 K, but no phase transition has been reported
at such high temperatures. The MD simulations also indicated that the 4c
site is the most stable site for the Na+ ions; the number of 4c sites is equal
to the number of Na+ ions, implying that some Na+ ions have to occupy
the slightly higher energy 6g sites for avoiding a strongly correlated
diffusion. Therefore, the most probable diffusion pathway for the Na+
ions is 4c-6g-4c89. The presence of Na+ ions in the higher energy sites
suggests a cooperative mechanism of Na+ ion migration.

Key challenges and prospects. These materials face several obstacles,
including but not limited to low ionic conductivity under ambient con-

penetrable rigid framework layers, structural instability stemming from
larger inter-layer separations, and volumetric alterations during ion
migration. Conversely, a weakly connecting framework affords facile
adjustments of the inter-layer spacing, a parameter that exhibits expo-
nential dependence on ionic diffusion. Additionally, the doubled-layered
honeycomb oxides represent a relatively new area.

reported in last decades among the sulﬁdes. This discovery represented a
signiﬁcant breakthrough in solid-state fast ion conductors and prompted
further exploration of these materials for application in ASSBs. The
LGPS-type material has a general formula of Li4−xGe1−xPxS4, where
x = 2/3. The crystal structure can be described as a tetragonal cell with
space group P42/nmc. The framework structure consists of (Ge0.5P0.5)S4,

debate. The origin of the high ionic conduction exhibited by LGPS-type
materials has been extensively investigated through theoretical studies.

lective ion migrations along the 1D path facilitated by Coulombic
interactions among the lithium ions. This phenomenon, known as the
cooperative migration mechanism, involves the coordination and
movement of multiple Li+ ions within the available conduction pathways,
resulting in enhanced ionic conductivity.

Complex hydrides. In SFIC, the comparison between β-alumina and
NASICONs has been a subject of scientiﬁc exploration. However, it was
not until 2007 that hydride materials, which have been studied for
hydrogen storage, were recognized for their potential as fast ion
conductors156. A pivotal discovery was made when it was found that Li+
ions exhibited signiﬁcant conduction behavior in LiBH4 after a structural
transition from orthorhombic to hexagonal at 380 K with an approximate
conductivity of 10−3 S cm−1157–159. This breakthrough propelled the
research focus towards hydrides and their derivatives as solid electrolytes
for ASSBs, owing to their remarkable ionic conductivity, high thermal
and chemical stability, lightweight nature, stability against metal anode,
and favorable mechanical properties, making them an ideal interface
between the electrode and electrolyte.
In light of the promising characteristics exhibited by hydrides, it is
imperative to address several formidable challenges before realizing their
practical utility, with a notable concern being their relatively deﬁcient ionic
conductivity at RT157. Taking lithium borohydride (LiBH4) as an illustrative
example, it is evident that its RT ionic conductivity is signiﬁcantly limited,
being three to four orders of magnitude lower than desired157. However, a
phase transition occurs at elevated temperatures, speciﬁcally at 388 K,
leading to a remarkable augmentation in lithium-ion mobility, culminating
in an impressive conductivity value of 5 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 423 K156,158–160. This
underscores the pivotal role played by phase transitions within this class of
materials. Consequently, it is paramount to gain a comprehensive under-
standing of the intricacies surrounding phase transition phenomena,
encompassing the underlying factors and origins.
This improved ionic conductivity in LiBH4 arises due to the rotational
disorder of the [BH4]−units in the high-T phase, a unique characteristic of
such materials. The cationic sites’ lattice disorder contributes to the high
ionic conductivity, and the rotational disorder of BH4 units has been
investigated through techniques such as synchrotron X-ray powder dif-
fraction, QENS, and ab initio MD simulations161–164. Driven by the objective
of achieving high ionic conductivity, various efforts have been dedicated to
stabilizing the highly conductive phase at or near RT. One approach
involves doping halides into LiBH4, resulting in compounds such as
Li(BH4)1−x Xx (X = Cl, Br, and I), where an RT high conducting phase is
observed for x > 0.25 (0 < 1), particularly in the case of iodine doping156.

Lithium argyrodites. In pursuit of developing efﬁcient solid electrolytes
for advanced battery technologies, signiﬁcant research has been dedi-
cated to thiophosphate-based materials. Among these, lithium argyr-
odites have emerged as a highly researched class, with promising ionic
conductive properties reaching up to 24 mS cm−1182. However, it is
important to note that these materials often exhibit varying degrees of
disorder, particularly in the form of anion exchange between halogens
and sulfur sites. This anion exchange has a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the
cationic transport properties, prompting numerous studies aimed at
understanding the impact of iso- and aliovalent substitutions on this
exchange process. Nevertheless, altering the chemical composition
introduces complexities in comprehending the resultant transport
properties, thereby making it challenging to isolate individual inﬂuences.
Arising from the general formula Li7PS6, argyrodites display a transi-
tion from a normal to a superionic phase (orthorhombic to a cubic phase,
featuring the space group F43m). Their RT ionic conductivity remains
relativelylow, with valuesaround 1.6 × 10−6 S cm−1183. The high-conducting
phase canbe stabilized byintroducingcompositionssuch asLi6PS5X (where
X represents a halogen atom like Cl, Br, I, or a vacancy). In the cubic phase,
Li+ ions occupy 24g and 48h sites, S atoms are found in 4a and 4c sites, P
atoms are situated in 4b sites, and either halide atoms (X) or vacancies
occupy 4a sites. Speciﬁcally, the S atoms at 4a sites form PS4 tetrahedra with
P atoms at 4b sites, while the S atoms at 4c sites bridge between PS4 tetra-
hedra (as depicted in Fig. 7a)184–188. The Li ions are distributed in octahedral
coordination around the S atoms. The presence of various interstitial sites
surrounding the halide ions facilitates high ionic conductivity (Fig. 7c),
typically within the range of 10−2–10−3 S cm−1 at RT, with low activation
energies typically around 0.2 to 0.3 eV189.
Among the single halide argyrodites, Li6PS5Cl exhibits the highest
ionic conductivity at ambient temperature, ~10−3 S cm−1, followed by
Li6PS5Br with around 10−4 S cm−1, while Li6PS5I displays the lowest ionic
conductivity, ~10−6 S cm−1, as illustrated in Fig. 7b. The origin of such high
ionic conductivity in Li6PS5Cl and Li6PS5Br has been the focus of several
investigations. It has been observed that disorder involving S2−/X−site
occupancy plays a signiﬁcant role, resulting in ionic conductivity that is
three ordersof magnitude lower infully ordered Li6PS5I. On the other hand,
ref. 190 argued that an increase in Li-ion concentration and Li-site disorder
is the primary contributing factor.
A comprehensive investigation into bulk conductivity within the
argyrodite material Li6PS5Cl was conducted, employing a combination of
7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments and DFT-based MD
simulations by ref. 191. This study found two distinct lithium-ion diffusion
mechanisms within this material: (1) Local hops: The study revealed that
Li+-ions within Li6PS5Cl undergo local hops within the cage-like structures
formedbytheirpositionsaroundthesulfur(S)orchlorine(Cl)atoms.These
local hops represent a crucial aspect of Li+-ion conductivity within the
material. (2) Inter-Cage hops: In addition to the local hops, the study
identiﬁed inter-cage hops as a Li+-ion diffusion within the crystalline bulk
structure of Li6PS5Cl. This implies that the movement of Li+-ions between
different cage-like structures is a critical factor inﬂuencing the overall
lithium-ion conductivity in this material.

General understanding and common features
The development of solid ion conductors has primarily relied on the ion
conduction mechanism described by Eq. (6), where ion movement occurs
through hopping within a crystalline lattice11,211. Many structural families
have demonstrated reasonably high Li-ion conductivity at RT, with the
ability to vary Li-ion conductivity by several orders of magnitude within the
same crystal framework structures10,211. Therefore, this section is dedicated
to understanding the common and unique characteristics of the fast ion-
conducting materials examined in this review.
Coordination number
Early studies found that the conditions in which the high ionic conductivity
is realized with monovalent cation in a four- (tetrahedral) and three-
coordinated conﬁgurations with the anions212. Recent studies have expan-
ded this idea to link the high ionic conductivity observed in lithium sulﬁdes
(e.g., LGPS and Li7P3S11) to bcc sulfur-sublattice, which can lead to
energetically-equivalent tetrahedral (four-coordinated) sites of lithium and
large transport channels11.
Collective motion
Several studies revealed that the collective (or cooperative) transport of
lithium ions can ﬂatten the migration energy barrier, thereby facilitating
rapid ion conduction in materials like LGPS, cubic LLZO, and
NASICONs152,213,214.Inthesematerials,ithasbeenobserved that,onaverage,
two to three Li-ions migrate simultaneously45,111,215. For instance, Burbano
et al.100 identiﬁed the presence of string-like ion clusters that engage in
cooperative motion within LLZO.Similarly, a ring-like mechanism was also
reported in Na2LiFeTeO6
98. On the contrary, some studies reported that the
strong cation-cation correlation is responsible for slow cation diffusion100.
Hence, lower activation energy due to the collective diffusion of ions is
subject to further investigation.

Introducing anharmonicity
Anharmonicity is the deviation of a system from being a harmonic oscil-
lator, which means that the restoring force is not proportional to the dis-
placement of the system (Fig. 8f). Anharmonicity plays an important role in
ion conduction. Several theoretical and experimental studies (DFT calcu-
lations and neutron scattering experiments) have been performed recently
to understand the phonon modes that are coupled most strongly with ionic
conductivity and assess the role of anharmonicity. It has been ascertained
that the anharmonicity inherent in the phonon vibrational modes of the
host lattice exhibits substantial coupling with cationic diffusion. This phe-
nomenon has been notably observed in AgI224. The recognized strong
coupling between mobile ions and the host lattice holds pivotal importance
for the diffusion process. Furthermore, investigations extended to materials
like AgCrSe2, CuCrSe2, Li7P3S11, Li10GeP2S12, β-Li3PS4, Li3YBr6, and
Li3ErCl6 have revealed analogous coupling phenomena (refs. 225,226).
Dimensionality
3D diffusion pathways are crucial for achieving high ion conductivity, as
they provide a lower possibility of blocking the ion conduction path.
Structures that possess 3D diffusion pathways11 often exhibit a correlation
with high ion conductivity, while lower dimensionalities227 often result in
reduced ionic transport due to the potential obstruction of diffusion
channels by defects228, even though the conductivity eq. (6) tells a different
story, as the dimension term is in the denominator.
Optimal bottleneck radius
Ionic conductors with intrinsically low activation barriers are highly
desirable. SFICs exhibit ionic conduction through polyhedral faces, leading
to dynamic changes in the coordination environment of the migrating ion.
These transitions create structural bottlenecks (BNs) within the diffusion
pathway, directly inﬂuencing the activation energy barriers associated with
ion migration229. Edge-sharing polyhedra typically present less favorable
pathways for ion transport compared to face-sharing conﬁgurations. This is
attributed to the generally smaller BNs, which encountered signiﬁcant
changes in the coordination environment during ion jumps in edge-sharing
structures230. Structural modiﬁcations arising from substitutions can frus-
trate energy landscapes175,231,232, which impacts changing BNs radius233,234
(see Fig. 2a), consequently changing of the migration mechanism11,111.

BN does not necessarily guarantee enhanced ion conduction134. In many
cases, an optimal, moderate BN size proves efﬁcient ion transport, con-

Framework modiﬁcation
Aliovalent substitutions alter the lattice size and the charge carrier
density199,233,235. Since most superionic conductors feature several empty or
partially ﬁlled crystallographic sites236,237, these substitutions impact ionic
conductivity. These approaches are primarily concerned with crystal-

speciﬁcally weakening the bonding between the mobile cations and sur-
rounding anions238,239. Consequently, the lowered activation barrier facil-
itates the departure of a cation from a stably coordinated lattice site, as less
energy is required. This decrease in bond strength, resulting in a ﬂatter
energy landscape for the ion jump240,241. This explains why sulﬁde ionic
conductors often exhibit a lower activation energy barrier and higher ionic
conductivity compared to most of the oxides242,243. Several notable examples

Enlarged Li-site
He etal.244 presentedan in-depthanalysis of the role of enlarged Li sitesin Li
superionic conductors. This was supported by AIMD simulations, which
revealed that Li-ions are situated in an elongated, asymmetrical probability
density. Rietveld reﬁnement derived from diffraction experiments
demonstrated that expanded Li sites exhibit high anisotropy and a large
atomic displacement parameter. These sites are frequently modeled as
multiple, partially occupied, closely neighboring sites within LGPS, garnet
LLZO, and argyrodite. Expanded Li sites, characterized by substantial local
spaces within the crystal structural frameworks of SICs, can accommodate
Li ions at various positions. This unique feature leads to positional dis-
ordering and increased conﬁgurational entropy of the Li-sites, thereby
signiﬁcantlydiminishingthe activation energybarrierand enhancingLi-ion
conduction. They also proposed that Li-sites’ disordering facilitates the
concerted migration of multiple Li ions, a mechanism often observed
in SFICs.
Activated diffusion network
The activated diffusion network (ADN) model offers a framework for
understanding ion conduction in speciﬁc lithium oxide SICs reported by
ref. 144. Inspired by highly conductive materials like garnets and NASI-
CONs, the ADN model postulates that most of the pristine SFICs exhibit
hindered Li-ion movement due to high migration barriers. However,
introducing excess Li-ions creates localized regions with signiﬁcantly
reducedbarriersthroughcooperativemotion245.Theseactivated regionscan
propagate throughout the network, leading to percolating Li-ion conduc-
tion.Themodelidentiﬁestwokeyrequirementsforfastionic conduction:1)
initiation, where excess Li-ions trigger the formation of activated
environments, and 2) propagation, where the activated state spreads
throughout the network without dying out and eventually percolates. Fur-
thermore, the ADN model highlights three beneﬁcial structural features for
superionic conductivity: 1) a 3D percolating Li-diffusion network, 2) short
distances between occupied Li sites, and 3) homogeneity of the transport
path. Leveraging these features as screening criteria, the authors propose
seven new lithium oxide SIC candidates with promising high room-
temperature ionic conductivity.

Corner-sharing framework
While ref. 246 examined structural commonalities among the fast Li-ion
conductors like Li1+xAlxTi2−x(PO4)3 (NASICON-type) and LiTa2PO8.

Na-designing principles
While Li-ion and Na-ion conductors share similar chemistry, their optimal
design principles differ. Unlike Li-ion conductors, Na-ion conductors
beneﬁt from face-sharing high-coordination number (CN) sites in their
diffusion channels. This unique feature, proposed by ref. 46, is exempliﬁed
by Na β-alumina and P2-type NaCoO2. Both materials possess face-sharing
prismatic Na sites with large bottleneck sizes, enabling direct Na+ hopping
with low energy barriers (0.26 eV in P2-type NaCoO2). This contrasts with
structures lacking face-sharing, like O3-type NaMO2, which exhibit high
energy barriers (0.76 eV) due to unfavorable intermediate sites. The face-
sharing arrangement facilitates efﬁcient Na+ movement by avoiding these
energy-intensive pathways. Therefore, in designing high-performance Na-
ion conductors, incorporating face-sharing high-CN sites emerges as a
critical strategy. This principle joins other established design elements like
largeNa+ sites,3Ddiffusionnetworks,and astronginductiveeffect,offering
a valuable tool for developing next-generation Na-ion battery materials.

Conclusions and future directions
In conclusion, we focused on the major classes of fast ion conductors,
such as layered oxides, polyhedral connecting frameworks, and cluster
anion-type materials, and a comprehensive exploration of their frame-
work structure on ion conduction mechanisms, and strategies for
enhancing ionic conductivity have been undertaken. The pivotal roles
played by experimental and theoretical approaches in unraveling the
atomistic insights of these materials have been highlighted. Our analysis
revealed that fast ion conduction is a complex phenomenon that depends
on various factors, such as structural bottlenecks, framework con-
nectivity, carrier concentration, anion rotation, cation-cation correlation
etc. Furthermore, mono-valent and di-valent cations need different
structural features for fast ion conduction. Based on a systematic
understanding of these factors, we suggest general principles and
guidelines for designing and optimizing fast ion conductors.
Future research in solid-state ion conductors should explore avenues
that enhance ion mobility beyond the current limitations. Three promising
strategies include:
Harnessing anion rotation and neutral molecule incorporation
Materials exhibiting anion rotation and integrating neutral molecules offer
signiﬁcant potential for achieving exceptional ionic conductivity. This is
particularly impactful for materials already possessing good ionic con-
ductivity, potentially pushing their performance to new heights.
Alloying two different materials
The successful implementation of combining distinct closo-type complex
hydrides exempliﬁes the efﬁcacy of this approach and encourages its
application to other classes of materials. Alloying offers a unique pathway to
tailor material properties by leveraging the synergistic effects between dif-
ferent components. This can lead to novel materials with enhanced ionic
conductivity, surpassing the limitations of individual constituents. For
instance, alloying can optimize the energy landscape for ion migration. By
carefully selecting and combining materials with complementary ionic
conductivities and activation energies, researchers can create pathways that
facilitate smoother ion movement and reduce energy barriers. This strategy
can also enhance structural stability, prevent phase transitions and
decomposition that might hinder performance, and ﬁne-tune material
composition through alloying, allowing for the optimization of other
desirable characteristics, such as mechanical strength, thermal stability, and
electrochemical window.
